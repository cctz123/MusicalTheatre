## The thesis

Broadway is a historical archive of American culture. I did not arrive at that claim in a seminar. I arrived at it by sitting in the dark, year after year, and later noticing that the shows were arguing with one another—and with the country outside the theatre.

The origin story is simple. I saw my first Broadway production at three. The exhibition is what that habit became once I also became interested in history, journalism, technology, and the ways people tell stories. Those interests did not pull me away from musicals. They gave me better questions to bring back into the house.

## Why these productions?

The 2026 research catalog holds 146 titles: precursors, Broadway landmarks, revivals, and works still entering the repertory. The exhibition galleries do not display all of them. Roughly eighty cards were chosen because they can carry a historical argument—not because they are my favorites.

Selection weighed several kinds of significance at once:

- Artistic form: did the production change what a musical could be (integration, concept, score, narrative structure)?
- Cultural pressure: did it make race, immigration, war, gender, class, or national myth newly visible on a commercial stage?
- Technical invention: did stagecraft, amplification, automation, puppetry, or digital scenery become part of the meaning?
- Afterlife: did later artists, revivals, or audiences keep arguing with it?

A title can be commercially huge and still thin as evidence. A title can be historically decisive and still sit outside my personal archive. Both facts matter. The catalog is the wider net. The galleries are the argument.

## What “I was there” means

Personal photographs mark nights I attended. “Attended Original” means I experienced the production the card is about. “Attended Revival” means the card is the original work, and the photograph is from a later staging I actually saw. Exhibition posters mark productions I did not see, or revivals for which no Playbill from my archive survives—those cards are Researched Original or Researched Revival. Official Playbill covers and production stills are not downloaded. The poster is an original object of the museum, not a missing file.

That split is part of the claim. Some Broadway history I inherited through research, recordings, and revivals. Some of it I walked into as an audience member. The two histories run at the same time. “I was there” remains the name of the personal archive; the four labels above are how each exhibition card is classified.

## Why a museum, not a book?

A book would flatten the material into one path. A database would flatten it into rows. Exhibition architecture lets a visitor move three ways through the same evidence: chronologically through the galleries, thematically through Gallery X, and at scale through the catalog. That is closer to how the history actually works. Musicals do not arrive in a single order of meaning.

The museum metaphor is also a research method. Wall labels, galleries, and cards force a choice: what belongs beside what, and why. Curation is the interpretation.

## How the research was done

Cards draw on production histories, published criticism, standard surveys of the American musical, the Internet Broadway Database, and the 2026 significance spreadsheet that underpins the catalog. Where I have a personal Playbill or photograph, that object is primary evidence of attendance—not of the original production’s design.

Dates, credits, original-versus-revival distinctions, and claims of “firsts” are the places a theatre historian should be hardest on this site. The catalog is a working ledger. It will keep being corrected.

## Research and production

I conceived the information architecture, researched and curated the material, structured the catalog, and wrote the exhibition’s interpretive frame. The site was built with contemporary programming tools, including AI-assisted development in Cursor. The argument, the selection, and the personal archive are mine. The software is a means of making the museum public.
