---
title: Epilogue
---

I attended my first Broadway show when I was three. I could not have explained what made it special. I only knew that when the lights dimmed and the orchestra started, the room changed.

Those evenings became a private calendar. I can still place myself by which production I was waiting to enter: a child outside *Matilda*, later a teenager holding a Playbill under a marquee, later someone who left the theatre thinking about newspapers, machines, and who gets to tell a national story.

As I grew older I became interested in history, journalism, technology, and artificial intelligence—the ways people record what happened and the ways they invent what happens next. I expected those subjects to replace Broadway. They did the opposite. They sent me back with questions the three-year-old could not ask.

Why does a love story in *South Pacific* also have to decide what America is willing to say about race? Why does *Company* keep changing when the unmarried person at the center changes? Why does *Hamilton* feel like historiography set to rhythm? Why does a robot in *Maybe Happy Ending* make memory feel newly fragile?

I did not become someone who studies culture *instead* of someone who loves musicals. The two are the same habit, pointed at different objects. A reporter asks who was in the room. A historian asks what the room wanted to believe. A technologist asks what the machinery is doing to the people inside it. Broadway puts all three questions on a stage and charges admission.

That is why this exhibition exists. Not to prove that I have seen a lot of theatre. The photographs already do that work. It exists because the girl in those pictures grew into someone who could not look at a musical without also looking at the country that made it—and because Broadway, unlike a finished textbook, is still rewriting the next chapter in public.

The conversation is unfinished. I am still in it.

— Ciana
