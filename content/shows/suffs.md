## Overview

When Suffs opened at the Music Box Theatre on April 18, 2024, it brought the final years of the American women's suffrage movement to Broadway while asking a distinctly contemporary question: how do movements create change when the people fighting for the same goal disagree about how to achieve it? (Tony Awards) The musical begins in 1913, as young activist Alice Paul enters a movement already decades old. Paul is impatient with the gradual strategy of established suffrage leader Carrie Chapman Catt. She wants confrontation, national action, and a constitutional amendment. Around them are activists including Lucy Burns, Inez Milholland, Doris Stevens, Ruza Wenclawska, Ida B. Wells, and Mary Church Terrell, whose different experiences reveal that “the women's movement” was never a single unified movement.

Black suffragists challenge white leaders who expect them to subordinate racial equality to the campaign for women's voting rights. Younger activists challenge older leaders. Protesters debate compromise against confrontation. Friendships fracture under the pressure of political work. Songs including “Finish the Fight,” “Wait My Turn,” “The March,” “Worth It,” “The Young Are at the Gates,” “Insane,” “I Was Here,” and “Keep Marching” transform the suffrage movement from a simple story of heroic progress into something messier: people fighting over how to change history while they are still inside it. (IBDB)


## Historical Era

Suffs focuses primarily on the final phase of the long American struggle for women's voting rights, beginning with the 1913 Woman Suffrage Procession in Washington, D.C. and moving toward ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. The amendment prohibited denying the right to vote on the basis of sex, but its passage did not mean that all American women could freely vote. Black women, particularly in the South, continued to confront poll taxes, literacy tests, intimidation, violence, and other mechanisms of disenfranchisement. Native American women faced questions of citizenship, while many Asian immigrants remained excluded from citizenship and therefore voting rights. That makes 1920 simultaneously a monumental victory and an incomplete one. Suffs deliberately builds itself around that contradiction. Winning something is not the same as finishing the work. Alice Paul and Carrie Chapman Catt One of the musical's strongest choices is refusing to portray political movements as unified. Alice Paul and Carrie Chapman Catt want the same fundamental outcome but disagree intensely over strategy. Catt represents an older generation of suffrage leadership and favors a more cautious political approach, including state-by-state campaigns and relationships with political leaders. Paul wants a federal constitutional amendment and is willing to use increasingly confrontational tactics. Their disagreement reflects a real strategic division within the movement. Historians have noted that the conflict between Paul's younger activists and Catt's established organization provides one of the musical's strongest historical foundations. (Smithsonian Magazine)

The musical therefore rejects the simplified version of activism in which courageous reformers stand together against obvious opponents. Sometimes the hardest arguments happen among people who agree about the destination but disagree about the road. Race and the Limits of the Suffrage Movement Suffs also confronts one of the most important contradictions within the movement: white suffragists could demand equality for themselves while participating in or accommodating racial discrimination. This tension becomes especially visible through Ida B. Wells, played in the original Broadway production by Nikki M. James. Wells was not simply a suffragist. She was a journalist, educator, and one of America's most important anti-lynching activists. The musical places her in conflict with Alice Paul over racism within suffrage organizing, including the controversy surrounding Black women's participation in the 1913 Washington procession. The precise historical details surrounding who directed Wells and other Black suffragists where to march are more complicated than a musical can fully represent, and historians continue to scrutinize the surviving evidence. (Alice Paul Center) That complication is important rather than inconvenient. Suffs asks audiences to recognize that a movement can be historically transformative and still reproduce the exclusions of the society it is trying to change. Activism as Process Where Hamilton often emphasizes extraordinary individuals shaping institutions, Suffs is particularly interested in the mechanics of organizing. People write letters. They hold meetings. They argue over tactics. They plan demonstrations. They lobby politicians. They raise money.

They endure criticism. They march. They picket. They are arrested. Some imprisoned activists undertake hunger strikes and are force-fed. Change arrives not through one heroic moment but through the accumulation of thousands of actions—many repetitive, frustrating, or initially unsuccessful. This makes Suffs unusually interested in something historical narratives often compress: the work between the famous events. “Wait My Turn” The idea of waiting appears repeatedly throughout the musical. For marginalized groups, demands for equality are often answered with some variation of the same argument: Not yet. The timing is wrong. The country is not ready. Another issue is more urgent. Be patient. The musical turns that language into a central political question. Who gets to decide when someone else has waited long enough? But Suffs complicates even this idea. Alice Paul refuses to wait for women's suffrage while Black women are simultaneously being asked by parts of the suffrage movement to wait for racial equality. The person demanding urgency in one struggle can become the person demanding patience in another. That contradiction is one of the show's most important historical insights. Production and Storytelling

The Broadway production used a cast composed of women and nonbinary performers, including performers portraying male historical figures. Unlike a conventional historical drama, Suffs does not attempt to disappear completely into realism. The performers tell history while simultaneously interpreting it. Shaina Taub herself originated the Broadway role of Alice Paul while also serving as the musical's sole book writer, composer, and lyricist—making the relationship between storyteller and historical subject particularly visible. (Shubert Organization) The production's theatrical vocabulary also emphasizes collective movement. Individuals step forward temporarily, but the ensemble remains essential. That structure mirrors the show's political argument: history may remember a handful of names, but movements are made by far more people than history usually names. Women Writing Women Back Into History Suffs belongs to an increasingly important Broadway tradition of revisiting familiar historical narratives from perspectives previously pushed toward the margins. Hamilton asked who gets to embody the Founding Fathers. SIX took six women remembered primarily as Henry VIII's wives and handed them microphones. Suffs pushes that project in another direction. Its women are not primarily adjacent to famous men. They are political strategists themselves. They negotiate. They organize. They disagree. They fail. They reconsider. They build institutions. And importantly, Taub does not attempt to make all of them uncomplicated heroes.

That choice matters because genuine historical inclusion should mean giving women access not only to heroism but to complexity.


## Historical Significance

Suffs originally premiered at the Public Theater in 2022 before being substantially revised for Broadway. The Broadway production opened at the Music Box Theatre in April 2024 and ultimately ran through January 5, 2025. (Shaina Taub) At the 2024 Tony Awards, it received six nominations, including Best Musical. Shaina Taub won both Best Book of a Musical and Best Original Score. She became the first woman in Tony history to win both categories on her own for the same musical. (Tony Awards) That achievement gives Suffs an additional layer of historical meaning. A musical about women fighting to gain political voice was created by a woman exercising unusual creative control over the telling of their history. The form and subject reinforce one another. “Keep Marching” The musical could easily have ended in 1920. The Nineteenth Amendment is ratified. The suffragists win. Curtain. Instead, Suffs deliberately complicates the victory. The final number, “Keep Marching,” connects one generation of activism to the generations that follow. The message is not that nothing changed. Enormous change occurred. But political victories do not permanently resolve the conflicts that produced them. New exclusions become visible. New generations question the compromises of earlier ones. Strategies that once seemed radical become institutions that younger activists eventually challenge. Progress therefore becomes less like a finish line than a relay. One generation moves the struggle forward and then hands it to another.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What I find most powerful about Suffs is that it refuses to make history look cleaner than it actually was. The suffragists share an enormous goal, but they disagree about strategy, generation, race, leadership, and whose rights should receive priority. That makes the movement feel much more human—and much more useful to study. I especially like the tension between “Wait My Turn” and “Keep Marching.” History is full of people being told that change should happen later, while every generation eventually discovers that its own victory has left something unfinished. For me, Suffs captures why history matters in the present: we inherit both the achievements and the limitations of the people who came before us. The responsibility of the next generation is not simply to celebrate how far they marched, but to decide where the march needs to go next.
