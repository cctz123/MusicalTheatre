## Overview

When Once opened at the Bernard B. Jacobs Theatre on March 18, 2012, it offered an unusually intimate alternative to the spectacle that had defined many modern Broadway blockbusters. There was no helicopter, falling chandelier, flying witch, or enormous ensemble. Instead, there was a group of performers holding their own instruments and a simple story about two people who meet through music and change each other without necessarily ending up together. (IBDB) Set in Dublin, the musical centers on two characters identified simply as Guy and Girl. Guy is an Irish street musician and vacuum-repairman who is ready to abandon his music. Girl is a Czech immigrant,

pianist, and young mother who hears him perform and immediately recognizes something in his songs that he has stopped recognizing himself. She persuades him not to give up. Over the course of several days, they write, rehearse, and record music together while gradually developing feelings for one another. But both have unfinished relationships elsewhere: Guy remains emotionally connected to a former girlfriend, while Girl is separated from her husband. Songs including “Falling Slowly,” “If You Want Me,” “Gold,” “When Your Mind’s Made Up,” and “The Hill” allow their relationship to develop through musical collaboration rather than conventional romantic declarations. The result is a love story in which the most important outcome is not who ends up with whom, but what two people enable each other to become.


## Historical Era

Once arrived on Broadway in 2012, at a time when large-scale productions and recognizable entertainment properties had become central to the commercial theater. Wicked and The Lion King remained major attractions. The Book of Mormon had become a blockbuster comedy the previous season, while Disney’s Newsies opened on Broadway only weeks after Once. Against this environment, Once was strikingly restrained. It had begun at the New York Theatre Workshop, the same Off-Broadway institution where Rent had developed years earlier, before transferring to Broadway. (Playbill) Its success demonstrated that Broadway spectacle did not necessarily require enormous scenery or advanced stage technology. Sometimes the unusual theatrical event could be something much simpler: watching people make music together in the same room.


## Historical & Cultural Context

Once originated as a low-budget 2007 Irish independent film, written and directed by John Carney and starring musicians Glen Hansard and Markéta Irglová. Its most famous song, “Falling Slowly,” won the Academy Award for Best Original Song. The stage adaptation faced an interesting problem. The film's appeal depended partly on intimacy and naturalism. Simply enlarging it into a conventional Broadway musical could have destroyed precisely what made it distinctive.

Enda Walsh's stage adaptation therefore did not attempt to make the story bigger simply because Broadway theaters were bigger. Instead, the creative team made the process of creating music more visible. This allowed Once to become theatrical without becoming spectacular in the conventional sense. Immigration, Dublin, and Belonging Girl's identity as a Czech immigrant in Ireland gives the musical another important dimension. She lives with her daughter and extended Czech family, moving between languages and cultural communities while building a life in Dublin. Guy, despite being Irish, is also uncertain about whether Dublin remains where his future belongs. Both characters are therefore negotiating different versions of belonging. Girl has physically left one country and created a life in another. Guy has remained physically at home but feels emotionally and creatively stuck. Their relationship temporarily creates a third kind of space—one built around music rather than nationality. That gives Once a quiet argument about cultural connection: people do not have to share the same background to recognize something in one another.


## Production Innovation

The defining theatrical innovation of Once was its actor-musician ensemble. There was no conventional orchestra hidden in a pit. The performers themselves played the instruments throughout the production—guitars, violins, piano, mandolin, accordion, percussion, and others. (Evamere Entertainment) When characters were not at the center of a scene, performers could remain visible and become part of the musical accompaniment. That erased the usual boundary between character, actor, and musician. The production also used Bob Crowley's relatively simple pub setting as its central environment. Rather than constantly replacing scenery to tell audiences where they were, performance and imagination transformed the space. Even before the formal performance began, the theatrical environment encouraged a sense of communal music-making.

The result felt closer at moments to entering an Irish pub where musicians happened to gather than watching a conventional Broadway orchestra accompany actors from somewhere unseen. Music as Action In many musicals, characters experience something and then sing about it. In Once, making music is often the thing that is happening. Guy and Girl become close because they rehearse together. Their relationship develops while writing and recording songs. Other characters become part of their community by joining the band. This distinction makes “Falling Slowly” especially important. The song is beautiful on its own, but within the musical its emotional power comes from watching two people discover how their voices and instruments fit together. Music becomes a form of communication for feelings they cannot express as easily through ordinary conversation. The audience is therefore not simply hearing the soundtrack to a relationship. We are watching the relationship being created through the soundtrack. Love Without the Conventional Ending Once also quietly challenges one of musical theater's oldest expectations: that a central romantic relationship should culminate in the couple ending up together. Guy and Girl clearly change each other. But the musical does not suggest that emotional significance automatically creates romantic entitlement. Girl has responsibilities and relationships of her own. Guy eventually reconnects with the possibility of another future. Their relationship matters precisely because it does not need to become permanent in order to have been transformative. That makes Once unusual among Broadway romances. Sometimes the person who changes your life is not necessarily the person with whom you spend it.


## Historical Significance

The original Broadway production starred Steve Kazee as Guy and Cristin Milioti as Girl. It received 11 Tony Award nominations and won eight, the most of any production that season, including Best

Musical, Best Book, Best Actor for Kazee, Best Direction for John Tiffany, Best Orchestrations, Best Scenic Design, Best Lighting Design, and Best Sound Design. (Evamere Entertainment) Its commercial performance was equally impressive for such an intimate production. Producers announced that it had recouped its investment only 21 weeks into its Broadway run. (Playbill) Once ultimately played 1,168 Broadway performances, closing on January 4, 2015. (IBDB) Its success demonstrated that twenty-first-century Broadway did not have to choose between commercial viability and theatrical simplicity. A show could feel handmade and still become a major Broadway hit.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What I find most beautiful about Once is its refusal to treat every meaningful connection as something that has to last forever. Guy and Girl enter each other's lives briefly, but each leaves with possibilities that did not exist before they met. I also love the decision to keep the musicians visible: there is something especially powerful about watching performers create the music rather than having it arrive invisibly from an orchestra pit. For me, Once captures one of the things live performance does best—the sense that something meaningful can exist because a particular group of people happened to come together in one place, create something, and then eventually move on.
