## Overview

When Fun Home opened at Broadway’s Circle in the Square Theatre on April 19, 2015, it brought an unusually intimate form of autobiography to the Broadway musical. Based on cartoonist Alison Bechdel’s graphic memoir, the show explores sexuality, family, memory, and the difficulty of ever fully knowing another person—especially a parent. Alison appears onstage at three different ages simultaneously. Adult Alison, a successful cartoonist, attempts to reconstruct her childhood and understand her relationship with her father, Bruce. Medium Alison is a college student beginning to recognize and openly embrace her identity as a lesbian. Small Alison is a child growing up in Pennsylvania in the elaborate Victorian house her father obsessively restores. Bruce is an English teacher, funeral-home director, preservationist, husband, and closeted gay man who has relationships with younger men. Several months after Alison comes out to her parents, Bruce dies after stepping in front of an oncoming truck—an event Alison and her family understand as suicide. Songs including “Welcome to Our House on Maple Avenue,” “Changing My Major,” “Days and Days,” “Telephone Wire,” “Ring of Keys,” and “Flying Away” transform Alison’s attempt to understand her father into an investigation of memory itself. The musical’s central question is never completely answered: how do you understand someone you loved when parts of their life will always remain unknowable?


## Historical Era

Fun Home reached Broadway in 2015, during an important period of experimentation in American musical theatre. The decade was producing works that expanded both who could occupy the center of a Broadway musical and how those stories could be told. The Book of Mormon had pushed musical comedy toward extreme satire; Once embraced actor-musicians and intimacy; Matilda explored childhood through sophisticated theatrical storytelling. Later that same year, Hamilton would radically rethink the historical musical. Fun Home moved in another direction. Rather than becoming larger, it became smaller and more interior. The show had premiered Off-Broadway at the Public Theater in 2013 before transferring to Broadway's Circle in the Square, an unusually intimate theater where the audience surrounds the playing space. The physical arrangement was perfect for a musical built around looking backward into one family's memories.


## Historical & Cultural Context

Alison Bechdel published Fun Home: A Family Tragicomic in 2006. The memoir combines drawings, literary references, family documents, photographs, and personal reflection as Bechdel attempts to reconstruct both her own coming-of-age and her father's hidden life. The Broadway musical preserves that process of reconstruction. Adult Alison does not simply narrate events she remembers perfectly. She draws, revises, watches, interrupts, and searches for the right caption. That distinction matters because Fun Home is not presented as objective history. It is memory being assembled in front of us. The musical therefore asks questions familiar to historians as well as memoirists: What evidence survives? Which details matter? How does what we know now change the way we interpret what happened then? Three Alisons The musical’s most distinctive structural choice is dividing Alison among three performers. Small Alison experiences childhood without fully understanding what she sees. Medium Alison experiences the exhilaration and awkwardness of discovering her sexuality. Adult Alison knows what eventually happens but still cannot completely explain it. Because all three can exist onstage together, the musical breaks ordinary chronological time. Adult Alison can watch her younger self. The audience can understand significance that Small Alison cannot yet recognize. A childhood memory can suddenly acquire meaning decades later. The structure captures something difficult to represent realistically: we do not remember our lives as a simple chronological sequence. The person remembering and the person being remembered exist together. “Ring of Keys” and Representation One of the musical’s most important moments occurs when Small Alison sees a masculine-presenting woman delivering packages at a diner. Nothing conventionally dramatic happens.

The woman does not speak to Alison. They never form a relationship. But Alison recognizes something. In “Ring of Keys,” she struggles to explain why this stranger feels so important. She does not yet possess the adult vocabulary for sexual orientation or gender presentation. She simply recognizes a way of existing that feels connected to herself. That makes the song a remarkable depiction of representation before language. Small Alison does not learn who she is because someone explains it to her. She sees evidence that a person like the one she might become can exist in the world. Bruce and Alison The relationship between Alison and Bruce gives the musical its deepest complexity. Father and daughter are both gay, but they belong to different generations and respond very differently to that identity. Alison eventually comes out and builds an open life. Bruce remains closeted. The parallel makes it tempting to understand Bruce simply as a tragic version of the life Alison escaped, but the musical resists such an easy interpretation. His secrecy, anger, artistic sensitivity, controlling behavior, love for his children, and harmful choices exist simultaneously. Alison wants a clear explanation. She never gets one. In “Telephone Wire,” Adult Alison reconstructs a final car ride with her father, desperately imagining whether there was something either of them could have said. There is no grand confrontation. That absence is precisely what makes the scene devastating.


## Production Innovation

Director Sam Gold and the creative team used Circle in the Square's in-the-round configuration to create a production built around fragments rather than conventional scenic realism. Furniture and objects could appear from beneath the stage as memories surfaced.

Adult Alison often remained nearby, observing scenes from her own past like a researcher examining evidence. This relationship between theatrical space and memory became one of the production's defining innovations. Unlike The Phantom of the Opera, Wicked, or The Lion King, Fun Home did not use spectacle to make its world larger. It used theatrical technique to make interiority visible. Jeanine Tesori's score similarly moves among childhood playfulness, 1970s popular music, intimate character songs, and more complex musical structures. The sound changes because memory changes.


## Historical Significance

At the 2015 Tony Awards, Fun Home won five awards, including Best Musical, Best Book, Best Original Score, Best Direction, and Best Actor in a Musical for Michael Cerveris as Bruce. Its Best Original Score win was historically significant: Jeanine Tesori and Lisa Kron became the first all-female writing team to win the Tony Award for Best Original Score. The production also marked a major moment for LGBTQ+ representation on Broadway. Although queer characters had appeared in many earlier musicals—including Cabaret, La Cage aux Folles, Rent, and Falsettos—Fun Home placed a lesbian woman's autobiographical experience at the structural and emotional center of a Best Musical-winning Broadway work. Young Sydney Lucas's performance of “Ring of Keys” became one of the production's defining images. The Graphic Novel Becomes Theatre Fun Home also represents an unusual form of adaptation. A graphic memoir communicates through the relationship between image and text. Bechdel can draw a childhood scene while adult narration interprets it from decades later. The musical had to find a theatrical equivalent. Its solution was the three Alisons. Instead of placing past and present together on a printed page, theater places them together onstage. Adult Alison can literally watch the child she used to be.

The adaptation therefore demonstrates something central to Broadway's continuing evolution: adapting a work successfully does not mean reproducing its surface. It means finding what a different medium can do with the same underlying idea.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What I find most powerful about Fun Home is the way it treats memory almost like historical research. Adult Alison keeps returning to small moments—a photograph, a car ride, a stranger carrying a ring of keys—trying to understand what they mean with information she did not possess when they happened. Yet the musical refuses to give her a perfect answer about her father. That feels true to the way we understand both people and history: we collect evidence, reinterpret it as we learn more, and sometimes still have to live with uncertainty. “Ring of Keys” especially stays with me because it shows how powerful it can be simply to see a possible version of yourself in another person before you even have the words to explain why.
