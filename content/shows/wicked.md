## Overview

When Wicked opened at the Gershwin Theatre on October 30, 2003, it took one of American popular culture’s most recognizable villains and asked audiences to reconsider the story they thought they knew. Long before Dorothy arrives in Oz, Elphaba, a brilliant young woman born with green skin, meets the privileged and popular Galinda, later Glinda, at Shiz University. Their initial hostility develops into an unlikely friendship. But as Elphaba discovers that the Wizard’s government is stripping Oz’s talking Animals of their rights and voices, she refuses to cooperate. The same society that once considered her merely strange begins calling her “wicked.” Glinda chooses a different path, working within the political system while becoming an adored public figure. Their choices separate them, even as their friendship remains the emotional center of the musical. Songs including “The Wizard and I,” “Popular,” “Defying Gravity,” “No Good Deed,” and “For Good” transform the familiar world of Oz into a story about friendship, prejudice, political narratives, and identity. At its center is a deceptively simple question: Who gets to decide who is good and who is wicked?


## Historical Era

Wicked arrived on Broadway in 2003, as the industry was increasingly shaped by familiar cultural properties, large-scale commercial productions, and family audiences. Disney had established The Lion King and Beauty and the Beast as long-running successes. Film adaptations such as The Producers and Hairspray demonstrated the value of recognizable source material. Wicked participated in this trend while doing something different: it did not simply retell The Wizard of Oz. It reinterpreted a cultural myth from the perspective of the character previously labeled its villain. That approach anticipated a much larger twenty-first-century interest in revisionist storytelling: familiar stories retold from marginalized, misunderstood, or supposedly villainous perspectives. At the same time, Wicked combined this reinterpretation with the scale of the modern Broadway blockbuster—an enormous theater, elaborate costumes, spectacular scenery, theatrical effects, and a score designed to deliver major emotional moments.


## Historical & Cultural Context

The world of Oz had been embedded in American culture for more than a century. L. Frank Baum published The Wonderful Wizard of Oz in 1900, but for many audiences, the defining version became MGM’s 1939 film The Wizard of Oz, starring Judy Garland. In that story, the Wicked Witch of the West appears almost entirely as an antagonist. Gregory Maguire’s novel radically altered the perspective. What if the Witch had a childhood? Political beliefs? Relationships? What if the label “wicked” represented someone else’s interpretation rather than an objective truth? The Broadway musical softened and reshaped much of Maguire’s darker novel, placing Elphaba and Glinda’s friendship at its center. This changes the story fundamentally. Elphaba is not born wicked. She becomes politically inconvenient. Her green skin makes her visibly different before she has done anything wrong, while her defense of the Animals turns that difference into something the government can exploit. Politics, Propaganda, and Otherness One of Wicked’s most important ideas is that political power depends partly upon controlling stories. The Wizard initially appears warm and paternal. Elphaba has spent years imagining that meeting him will finally bring acceptance.

Instead, she discovers that his government is responsible for the persecution of Oz’s talking Animals. When she refuses to cooperate, the government does not simply pursue her. It defines her. Madame Morrible publicly announces that Elphaba is dangerous, and the population gradually accepts the image of the “Wicked Witch.” The musical therefore separates who Elphaba is from the story society tells about her. Glinda occupies the opposite position. She becomes “Glinda the Good,” a carefully constructed public symbol whose popularity gives political authority legitimacy. Neither label fully captures the woman underneath it.


## Production Innovation

Wicked uses the machinery of the Broadway spectacle to make Elphaba’s transformation physically visible. Eugene Lee’s scenic design created a fantasy world dominated by gears, towers, enormous moving structures, and the Time Dragon Clock. Susan Hilferty’s costumes developed a detailed visual language for Oz rather than simply reproducing the familiar imagery of the 1939 film. The production’s defining theatrical moment comes at the end of Act I. In “Defying Gravity,” Elphaba decides that acceptance is no longer worth the price of obedience. As the music builds, she rises above the stage while guards and citizens gather below. The effect is spectacular, but the spectacle works because it represents an internal decision. Elphaba does not suddenly gain power in that moment. She decides to stop asking permission to use the power she already has. That combination of character transformation, song, lighting, machinery, and physical elevation made “Defying Gravity” one of the defining Broadway moments of the twenty-first century.


## Historical Significance

The original Broadway production starred Idina Menzel as Elphaba and Kristin Chenoweth as Glinda, performances that became inseparable from the musical’s early identity. Menzel won the 2004 Tony Award for Best Actress in a Musical, while the production also won Tonys for scenic and costume design.

Although Wicked lost the Tony Award for Best Musical to Avenue Q, its subsequent commercial history became extraordinary. It developed into one of Broadway’s longest-running and highest-grossing productions, while international companies introduced the musical to audiences around the world. Its popularity also demonstrated the growing importance of fandom to twenty-first-century Broadway. Elphaba and Glinda became recognizable cultural figures beyond traditional theater audiences, and songs such as “Defying Gravity” entered popular culture independently of the production. The two-part film adaptation beginning with 2024’s Wicked expanded that cultural reach further, introducing the musical to another generation and demonstrating the increasingly circular relationship between literature, film, and Broadway: Baum’s novel inspired films; Maguire reinterpreted Baum; Broadway adapted Maguire; and Hollywood then adapted Broadway. Friendship at the Center Despite its politics and spectacle, Wicked ultimately depends on the relationship between two women. Elphaba and Glinda do not simply represent good and evil. They represent different responses to the world around them. Elphaba challenges systems directly, sometimes without understanding the consequences. Glinda initially benefits from those systems and compromises with them, but gradually recognizes the costs of doing so. Neither can become the person she eventually becomes without the other. That is why “For Good” matters as much as “Defying Gravity.” The musical’s final emotional statement is not about defeating a villain or winning a romance. It is about recognizing that another person has permanently changed you.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What I find most powerful about Wicked is the idea that the same person can become the hero of one story and the villain of another depending on who controls the narrative. Elphaba’s actions do not change nearly as dramatically as the public story surrounding her does. That feels especially relevant in a world where reputations can be created through headlines, social media, and repeated narratives. But what keeps Wicked from becoming only a political allegory is Elphaba and Glinda’s friendship. “For Good” suggests that our impact is not measured only by whether the world remembers us as good or wicked, but by the ways we permanently change the people who actually knew us.
