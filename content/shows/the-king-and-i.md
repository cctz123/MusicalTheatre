## Overview

When The King and I opened at the St. James Theatre on March 29, 1951, Rodgers and Hammerstein turned a nineteenth-century encounter between Britain and Siam into a musical about cultural difference, power, education, modernization, and the difficulty of understanding another society through one's own assumptions. The story follows Anna Leonowens, a British schoolteacher who travels to Siam—present-day Thailand—in the 1860s to educate the children and wives of King Mongkut. Anna and the King repeatedly clash over authority, gender, slavery, diplomacy, and cultural traditions. Yet their arguments gradually develop into mutual fascination and respect. Their relationship never becomes a conventional romance. Instead, the emotional center of the musical lies in what each learns—and fails to learn—from the other. Alongside their story is the tragedy of Tuptim, a young Burmese woman presented to the King who remains in love with Lun Tha. Songs including “Getting to Know You,” “Hello, Young Lovers,” “I Have Dreamed,” “Something Wonderful,” and “Shall We Dance?” helped make The King and I one of the defining works of Broadway's Golden Age.


## Historical Era

The King and I premiered in 1951, during a period when the United States was becoming increasingly engaged with Asia. The Korean War was underway, decolonization was transforming former European empires, and Cold War politics were reshaping Western relationships with countries across Asia. The musical, however, looks backward to Siam in the 1860s, during the reign of King Mongkut. Siam occupied an unusual position in nineteenth-century Southeast Asia: unlike many neighboring territories, it avoided formal European colonization while facing enormous diplomatic pressure from imperial powers. This context gives the King's concern with modernization real historical significance. He understands that Western governments may interpret unfamiliar customs as evidence that Siam is “uncivilized,” potentially providing justification for foreign intervention. Yet the musical presents this history primarily through Western sources. Its narrative ultimately descends from Anna Leonowens's own published accounts, whose accuracy historians have questioned. The gap between historical Siam and the Western imagination of Siam has therefore become central to understanding the musical.


## Historical & Cultural Context

At its heart, The King and I presents a conflict over who has the authority to define “civilization.”

Anna arrives believing strongly in British ideas about education, individual liberty, gender, and morality. The King wants Western knowledge because he believes it can strengthen Siam, but he resists the assumption that adopting Western ideas requires surrendering his authority or culture. Their arguments create some of the musical's most compelling scenes because neither character is simply encountering the other as an individual. Each carries an entire system of cultural assumptions. The musical's treatment of women makes these tensions particularly visible. Anna challenges the King's patriarchal authority, while Tuptim's story exposes the consequences faced by a woman with far less power to resist it. At the same time, the musical itself participates in the Western tradition of Orientalism—representing Asian societies as exotic, mysterious, authoritarian, or culturally opposite to the West. Modern criticism has therefore questioned whether a story apparently advocating cross-cultural understanding still places Western values at the center of its moral universe. That contradiction is essential to the show's history.


## Production Innovation

Rodgers and Hammerstein used the integrated musical to turn cultural negotiation into dramatic action. “Getting to Know You” makes Anna's relationship with the royal children visible through music. “A Puzzlement” gives the King an unusually vulnerable interior voice as he struggles with the contradictions of leadership and modernization. “Something Wonderful,” sung by Lady Thiang, complicates Anna's judgment of the King by describing a form of love built around recognizing someone's failures as well as his strengths. The show's most famous sequence, “Shall We Dance?”, transforms Anna and the King's intellectual and emotional tension into movement. Their polka begins as a lesson in Western social dancing but becomes increasingly intimate, allowing physical performance to express feelings that their relationship cannot openly resolve. Another major theatrical sequence is “The Small House of Uncle Thomas,” Tuptim's adaptation of Uncle Tom's Cabin. Presented as entertainment for British visitors, the ballet allows Tuptim to criticize slavery—and implicitly her own lack of freedom—through performance. The device creates theatre inside theatre: a character uses storytelling to say politically dangerous things she cannot say directly.


## Historical Significance

The original production became a major Broadway success, running for 1,246 performances and winning the Tony Award for Best Musical. It also created one of Broadway's most iconic performances: Yul Brynner as the King.

Brynner's association with the role became extraordinary. He returned to it repeatedly onstage and in the 1956 film adaptation, eventually playing the King thousands of times. His performance became so culturally dominant that the visual image of the King—particularly Brynner's shaved head—became almost inseparable from the musical itself. The show's revival history has also revealed changing approaches to race and representation. Productions have increasingly reconsidered casting, accents, costumes, choreography, and the depiction of Asian characters. The acclaimed 2015 Broadway revival, directed by Bartlett Sher and starring Kelli O'Hara and Ken Watanabe, again placed questions of cultural difference and representation at the center of discussions surrounding the work. For modern audiences, The King and I therefore exists in two histories simultaneously. It is a landmark Golden Age musical that attempted to dramatize cross-cultural understanding, and it is a Western representation of Thailand shaped by the assumptions of its creators and source material. Its continuing importance comes partly from that tension. A musical about learning to understand another culture also reveals how difficult it is to escape the cultural perspective from which a story is being told.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What interests me most about The King and I is that Anna and the King never completely resolve their differences. They learn from each other without becoming the same, and their relationship remains defined by both connection and misunderstanding. For me, that makes the musical especially valuable to revisit today: cross-cultural understanding is not simply about encountering difference, but also about recognizing the assumptions we bring to that encounter ourselves.
