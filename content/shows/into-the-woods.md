## Overview

When Into the Woods opened at the Martin Beck Theatre on November 5, 1987, Stephen Sondheim and James Lapine began with stories almost everyone already knew—and then asked what happens after “happily ever after.” The musical intertwines the stories of Cinderella, Jack and the Beanstalk, Little Red Riding Hood, and Rapunzel with an original story about a Baker and his Wife who desperately want a child. A Witch tells them that a curse has left them childless and sends them into the woods to collect four objects needed to reverse it. By the end of Act I, everything seems resolved. Cinderella has her prince. Jack has defeated the Giant. Rapunzel has escaped her tower. The Baker and his Wife have their child. Everyone appears to have received what they wished for. Then Act II begins. A second Giant enters the kingdom seeking revenge. Characters die. Marriages become complicated. Parents fail their children. Wishes produce consequences no one anticipated. Songs including “Giants in the Sky,” “Agony,” “No One Is Alone,” “Children Will Listen,” and the title song transform familiar fairy tales into an exploration of desire, responsibility, parenthood, community, and the consequences of getting what we think we want.


## Historical Era

Into the Woods premiered on Broadway in 1987, during a decade increasingly associated with the large-scale megamusical. Cats, Les Misérables, and The Phantom of the Opera offered sweeping scores and spectacular theatrical worlds designed for international audiences. Sondheim and Lapine moved in a different direction. Their musical begins with recognizable fairy-tale entertainment but gradually becomes something psychologically and morally complicated.

The work was also the second Broadway collaboration between Sondheim and Lapine after Sunday in the Park with George. Both musicals investigate what happens when a person's desires conflict with responsibilities toward other people. Yet Into the Woods approaches that question through stories embedded deeply in Western popular culture. Instead of inventing an unfamiliar world, Sondheim and Lapine take stories audiences think they understand and destabilize them from inside.


## Historical & Cultural Context

Fairy tales traditionally help cultures communicate ideas about danger, morality, adulthood, family, and social behavior. Into the Woods retains those functions while questioning their simplicity. Cinderella wishes to attend the festival, but once she wins her prince, she discovers that marriage does not automatically produce fulfillment. Jack's adventure with the Giant initially resembles heroic fantasy, but the second act asks audiences to consider the people harmed by his actions. Little Red Riding Hood survives the Wolf but emerges from the experience less innocent. The Baker and his Wife discover that wanting a child and raising one are entirely different challenges. The woods become the space where these characters encounter uncertainty. Outside the woods, they believe they know the rules. Inside, identities and moral categories become unstable. The musical repeatedly complicates the distinction between heroes and villains. Even the Witch, apparently the story's obvious villain, sometimes understands consequences more clearly than anyone else. As she observes in “Last Midnight,” everyone wants someone else to blame.


## Production Innovation

One of Sondheim and Lapine's greatest achievements is the musical's use of repetition with changed meaning. The phrase “I wish” begins the show. At first, wishing seems innocent—the basic mechanism of a fairy tale. By Act II, the audience understands that every wish changes the world around it. Musical ideas return in similar ways. The woods themselves are revisited, but characters who enter them the second time are no longer the people who entered them the first. The structure of the musical therefore becomes its argument: Act I gives us the fairy tale; Act II gives us the consequences.

Sondheim also writes different musical languages for different characters. The rapid patterns of the Witch, the romantic sweep of the Princes, and the conversational material of the Baker and his Wife create distinct personalities while remaining part of the same musical world. The hilarious “Agony” turns the fairy-tale prince into satire. Cinderella's Prince and Rapunzel's Prince compete over whose inaccessible woman causes greater suffering, revealing that they may be more in love with pursuit than partnership.


## Historical Significance

The original Broadway production starred Bernadette Peters as the Witch and Joanna Gleason as the Baker's Wife and ran for 764 performances. At the 1988 Tony Awards, Into the Woods competed against The Phantom of the Opera. Phantom won Best Musical, but Into the Woods received major awards including Best Original Score, Best Book of a Musical, and Best Actress in a Musical for Gleason. That contrast is revealing. The two shows represent very different possibilities for late-1980s Broadway: the monumental international spectacle of the megamusical and Sondheim's intricate, self-questioning approach to storytelling. Into the Woods became one of Sondheim's most frequently produced works, partly because its fairy-tale framework makes its complexity unusually accessible. The 2022 Broadway revival, which grew out of a New York City Center Encores! production and featured performers including Sara Bareilles, Brian d'Arcy James, Patina Miller, and Gavin Creel, demonstrated the show's continuing power with relatively simple staging that emphasized performers and storytelling. Its themes have also continued to expand with time. The musical can be read through parenthood, environmental responsibility, political leadership, grief, community, or simply the process of growing up. Its central lesson is not that people should stop wishing. It is that wishes create responsibilities.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What I love about Into the Woods is that it begins with stories I thought I already understood and then refuses to let them remain simple. “Happily ever after” becomes a beginning rather than an ending. The line “No one is alone” is especially meaningful to me because the musical does not mean that no one will ever feel lonely; it means that our choices are never completely isolated. What we wish for, pursue, or abandon affects people around us. Into the Woods ultimately makes growing up feel less like learning the correct answer and more like learning that there may be no perfect choices—but we are still responsible for the choices we make.
