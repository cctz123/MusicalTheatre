## Overview

When Pal Joey opened at the Ethel Barrymore Theatre on December 25, 1940, it asked Broadway audiences to follow a protagonist who was not particularly admirable. Joey Evans is ambitious, charming, selfish, manipulative, and determined to become a nightclub star. Instead of learning a moral lesson and transforming into a better person, Joey largely remains Joey. Set in Chicago's nightclub world, the musical follows Joey as he pursues success while becoming involved with two women: Linda English, a young woman who genuinely cares for him, and Vera

Simpson, a wealthy married woman who offers to finance the nightclub Joey dreams of running. His relationships become entangled with his ambition, and romance repeatedly gives way to questions of money, power, and self-interest. The Richard Rodgers and Lorenz Hart score included songs that became standards, particularly “Bewitched, Bothered and Bewildered,” “I Could Write a Book,” and “You Mustn’t Kick It Around.” But beneath the sophistication of the music was a strikingly cynical story. Pal Joey helped demonstrate that a musical did not need an innocent hero, idealized romance, or reassuring moral conclusion to hold an audience.


## Historical Era

Pal Joey appeared at a transitional moment in Broadway history. The spectacular revues and sophisticated musical comedies of the 1920s and 1930s were beginning to give way to the more dramatically integrated book musicals that would dominate the 1940s and 1950s. Only three years later, Rodgers would begin his partnership with Oscar Hammerstein II with Oklahoma!, helping usher in Broadway's celebrated Golden Age. Pal Joey, created with Rodgers's earlier collaborator Lorenz Hart, therefore stands close to the boundary between two eras. America itself was also approaching a turning point. The musical opened less than a year before the United States entered World War II. Rather than addressing international politics directly, however, Pal Joey presented a darker urban America of nightclubs, sexual relationships, social ambition, and economic dependence. Its world feels markedly different from the glamorous escapism of many earlier musical comedies.


## Historical & Cultural Context

The musical's most radical element was its moral ambiguity. Traditional musical-comedy protagonists were generally designed to win the audience's affection. Joey Evans was different. He wants success badly enough to manipulate the people around him, and his relationship with Vera is shaped openly by both sexual attraction and money. That gave Pal Joey an adult quality unusual for Broadway musical comedy. Its relationships are transactional as well as romantic. Wealth creates power. Charm becomes a tool. The nightclub itself represents a world in which identity can be constructed through performance. The show also reflects the increasingly sophisticated urban culture associated with writers such as John O'Hara, whose original Pal Joey stories appeared as fictional letters in The New Yorker. Their cynical voice carried into the musical. Rather than asking audiences simply to root for Joey, the show asks them to remain interested in him despite understanding exactly what kind of person he is.


## Production Innovation

Pal Joey was important because its songs were integrated not only with plot but with psychology and character. Rodgers and Hart's score rarely tells audiences that characters are morally better than their actions suggest. Instead, the music often exposes contradictions within them. The most famous example is “Bewitched, Bothered and Bewildered,” sung by Vera. It is a sophisticated love song, but its emotional power comes partly from the fact that Vera understands the imperfections of the man she desires. Romantic feeling is presented as complicated, physical, self-aware, and sometimes irrational. “I Could Write a Book,” by contrast, offers one of the score's most straightforward expressions of romance. Placed within Joey's morally unstable world, its apparent sincerity becomes even more striking. The show's integration therefore differs from Show Boat. Instead of using music primarily to support an epic narrative, Pal Joey uses songs to reveal psychological contradictions within characters who are neither purely heroic nor villainous.


## Historical Significance

The original production ran for 374 performances, a respectable run but not initially enough to establish the show as an unquestioned classic. Its reputation grew considerably through later reassessment. A major 1952 Broadway revival was especially important. By then, audiences had experienced works such as Oklahoma!, Carousel, and South Pacific, and Broadway had become more comfortable with dramatically ambitious musicals. Critics who had once found Pal Joey unusually cynical increasingly recognized how far ahead of its time it had been. Its legacy can be seen in later musicals centered on deeply flawed or morally ambiguous protagonists. Broadway would eventually produce characters such as Sweeney Todd, Sally Bowles, Bobby in Company, and the competing artists of Chicago—figures audiences are asked to understand without necessarily being asked to approve of them. Pal Joey helped establish that possibility decades earlier. Its historical importance therefore rests on a deceptively simple change in expectations: a musical's central character did not have to be a good person. Broadway could ask audiences to observe ambition, sexuality, selfishness, and compromise without resolving those qualities into a conventional moral lesson.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What interests me about Pal Joey is how modern its understanding of character feels. Joey does not fit neatly into the role of hero or villain, and the musical does not force him to. That ambiguity marks an

important development in Broadway storytelling: characters could become compelling because of their contradictions rather than despite them.
