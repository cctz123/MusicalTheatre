## Overview

When South Pacific opened at the Majestic Theatre on April 7, 1949, Rodgers and Hammerstein used the romantic musical to confront a subject unusually direct for postwar Broadway: racial prejudice. Set on islands in the South Pacific during World War II, the musical intertwines two relationships. Nellie Forbush, an American Navy nurse from Arkansas, falls in love with French plantation owner Emile de Becque, only to recoil when she discovers that his late wife was Polynesian and that his children are

biracial. At the same time, Lieutenant Joseph Cable falls in love with a young Tonkinese woman, Liat, but cannot imagine bringing her home and marrying her because of the racial attitudes he has inherited. Around these stories are sailors, nurses, military operations, comedy, and the looming danger of war. Songs including “Some Enchanted Evening,” “Bali Ha’i,” “There Is Nothin’ Like a Dame,” “I’m Gonna Wash That Man Right Outa My Hair,” and “You’ve Got to Be Carefully Taught” became some of Rodgers and Hammerstein's best-known work. The result is both a sweeping romantic musical and a critique of the prejudices that determine who is considered worthy of that romance.


## Historical Era

South Pacific premiered only four years after the end of World War II. For its original audience, the war was not distant history. Veterans had recently returned home, families were still processing loss, and the United States was beginning to define its role in the postwar world. At the same time, America remained deeply segregated. Jim Crow laws governed much of the South, interracial marriage remained illegal in many states, and the modern Civil Rights Movement had not yet reached its major victories. Rodgers and Hammerstein therefore used a recent and familiar American experience—the Pacific War—to examine a contradiction at home. The United States had fought a global war framed partly around freedom and democracy while maintaining systems of racial discrimination within its own society. The musical asks whether defeating an external enemy means very much if prejudice continues to shape people's most intimate decisions.


## Historical & Cultural Context

The central argument of South Pacific appears most explicitly in “You’ve Got to Be Carefully Taught.” After Cable realizes that his prejudice prevents him from marrying Liat, he rejects the idea that racial hatred is natural. Instead, he argues that people must be taught to fear and hate those considered different from themselves. For 1949 Broadway, the statement was provocative. Some critics and political figures objected to the song's message, but Rodgers and Hammerstein resisted pressure to remove it because the idea was central to the musical. Prejudice was not simply an unfortunate personality trait possessed by individual characters; it was something socially transmitted from one generation to another. Nellie's story makes that argument personal. She is cheerful, likable, and initially presented as an archetypal American heroine. Yet she carries prejudices learned from the society in which she grew up. Her racism therefore cannot be dismissed as the behavior of an obvious villain.

That choice makes the musical's critique more unsettling: prejudice can exist within otherwise sympathetic people. At the same time, South Pacific remains a product of its own era. Its representations of Pacific Islander and Asian characters, particularly Bloody Mary and Liat, have generated continuing criticism over exoticism, stereotypes, and the limited perspectives given to nonwhite characters. Like many Golden Age works, its progressive intentions coexist with representational limitations.


## Production Innovation

By South Pacific, Rodgers and Hammerstein had developed the integrated musical into an extraordinarily flexible dramatic form. Songs reveal character, advance relationships, establish place, and express ideas that dialogue alone could not communicate as effectively. “Some Enchanted Evening” gives Emile an immediate emotional directness, while “Bali Ha’i” transforms an island into a symbol of longing and possibility. The musical is especially notable for combining dramatically different tones. Comic ensemble numbers such as “There Is Nothin’ Like a Dame” exist alongside romance, wartime danger, death, and explicit discussions of racism. That mixture allows South Pacific to operate simultaneously as popular entertainment and social argument. The show's structure also links private decisions to larger political systems. Nellie's decision about whether she can accept Emile's children seems intensely personal, yet the musical reveals that her reaction has been shaped by an entire culture. Cable's relationship with Liat works similarly. Rodgers and Hammerstein therefore make romance political without abandoning the emotional appeal of the romantic musical.


## Historical Significance

South Pacific became one of the defining successes of Broadway's Golden Age, running for 1,925 performances in its original production. It achieved an extraordinary sweep of recognition. The musical received the 1950 Pulitzer Prize for Drama and dominated the Tony Awards, becoming the only musical to win all four acting categories at a single Tony Awards ceremony, in addition to Best Musical and other honors. Its cultural significance, however, extends beyond awards. By insisting that “You’ve Got to Be Carefully Taught” remain in the show, Rodgers and Hammerstein demonstrated that a commercially successful Broadway musical could take an explicit position on a controversial social issue. Entertainment and argument did not have to be separated.

The acclaimed 2008 Broadway revival, directed by Bartlett Sher and starring Kelli O'Hara and Paulo Szot, introduced the work to another generation and again brought attention to both its extraordinary score and its complicated racial politics. Today, South Pacific occupies an important place in Broadway history precisely because its progressiveness is not uncomplicated. It condemned racial prejudice unusually directly in 1949 while still representing Asian and Pacific Islander characters through assumptions inherited from its era. That contradiction makes it historically valuable. The musical demonstrates that Broadway can challenge the culture around it while still remaining shaped by that same culture.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What stays with me about South Pacific is the idea that prejudice is learned rather than inevitable. By placing that argument inside love stories instead of presenting it only as an abstract political issue, the musical shows how social beliefs become personal choices with real consequences. At the same time, its own limitations remind me that challenging one generation's assumptions does not necessarily free a work from all of them.
