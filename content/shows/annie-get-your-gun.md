## Overview

When Annie Get Your Gun opened at the Imperial Theatre on May 16, 1946, it transformed the life of legendary sharpshooter Annie Oakley into one of Broadway's defining Golden Age musical comedies. Written as a star vehicle for Ethel Merman, the show combined romance, spectacle, American mythology, and an Irving Berlin score that produced some of the most recognizable songs in musical theatre history. The musical follows Annie, a gifted but unsophisticated sharpshooter who joins Buffalo Bill's Wild West show after defeating its star marksman, Frank Butler, in a shooting contest. Annie quickly becomes a

celebrity—and falls in love with Frank. But her success threatens his pride, turning their romance into a competition over whether Annie can be both the better performer and the woman Frank wants to marry. Songs including “There’s No Business Like Show Business,” “Anything You Can Do,” “I Got the Sun in the Morning,” and “They Say It’s Wonderful” helped make the score one of Irving Berlin's greatest theatrical successes. Beneath its exuberant entertainment, however, Annie Get Your Gun raises questions about gender, celebrity, American identity, and the stories Broadway constructs from history.


## Historical Era

Annie Get Your Gun premiered in 1946, less than a year after the end of World War II. American society was entering a period of enormous transition as millions of servicemen returned home and the country attempted to shift from wartime mobilization to peacetime life. The musical's central conflict had particular resonance in that context. During the war, women had entered workplaces and taken on responsibilities traditionally associated with men. Afterward, cultural pressure encouraged many women to return to more conventional domestic roles. Annie's story contains a similar contradiction. She possesses extraordinary talent, earns public recognition, and repeatedly proves herself more capable than the men around her. Yet her romantic happiness ultimately depends on negotiating Frank's discomfort with being surpassed by a woman. The result reflects one of the central tensions of its era: how could female achievement coexist with traditional expectations of femininity?


## Historical & Cultural Context

The real Annie Oakley had been one of the most famous performers of the late nineteenth century. Born Phoebe Ann Moses in Ohio, she became internationally celebrated for her remarkable marksmanship and toured with Buffalo Bill's Wild West. The Broadway musical, however, is not a biography. It transforms Oakley's life into a romantic comedy and participates in a larger American mythology of the Wild West—a world of sharpshooters, cowboys, showmanship, competition, and supposedly limitless possibility. That mythology is important because Buffalo Bill's Wild West itself helped turn western expansion into mass entertainment. By the time Annie Get Your Gun reached Broadway, the frontier had already become less a historical place than an American cultural story. The musical also inherited troubling representations from that tradition. Its original portrayal of Native Americans relied heavily on stereotypes, particularly in “I’m an Indian Too.” Later productions increasingly recognized that such material could not simply be treated as harmless period comedy. This creates an important distinction between historical preservation and historical endorsement. Understanding Annie Get Your Gun means recognizing both the enormous appeal of its American mythology and the people that mythology distorted or excluded.


## Production Innovation

Annie Get Your Gun is less revolutionary in form than Oklahoma! or Carousel, but it demonstrates the extraordinary craftsmanship of the Golden Age musical comedy. Irving Berlin wrote songs that function simultaneously as character pieces and popular standards. “Anything You Can Do” turns Annie and Frank's romantic conflict into a musical competition, with each trying to outperform the other. “They Say It’s Wonderful” reveals a softer vulnerability beneath their rivalry. “There’s No Business Like Show Business” became something even larger. Originally performed within a story about joining Buffalo Bill's company, the song eventually escaped the musical entirely and became an unofficial anthem for the entertainment industry itself. The original production was also inseparable from Ethel Merman, whose commanding vocal style and comic presence defined Annie for Broadway audiences. Her performance demonstrates another important feature of musical-theatre history: sometimes a role and a performer become so closely connected that the original star helps determine how a character is understood for generations.


## Historical Significance

Annie Get Your Gun became one of the great commercial successes of the postwar Broadway era, running for 1,147 performances and strengthening Irving Berlin's status as one of the central architects of American popular song. Its historical importance also lies in Annie herself. She is unusually capable for a Golden Age heroine: professionally successful, economically productive, competitive, physically skilled, and repeatedly superior to her male romantic counterpart. Yet the musical ultimately struggles with the consequences of that independence. In the original story, Annie deliberately loses a shooting contest so that Frank's pride will be restored and their marriage can proceed. The resolution exposes the limits surrounding the show's otherwise powerful female protagonist. Later revivals have therefore done more than reproduce the original. The major 1999 Broadway revival starring Bernadette Peters substantially revised the book, reorganized the story, and removed or altered material considered offensive, including “I’m an Indian Too.” Its revised ending also allowed Annie and Frank to reach a more equal resolution. Those changes make Annie Get Your Gun especially useful for understanding the history of the Broadway revival. Reviving a musical can mean preserving its score while actively renegotiating the cultural assumptions built into its original story.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What interests me about Annie Get Your Gun is the contradiction at the center of Annie herself. She is celebrated because she is extraordinary, yet the original musical still asks how much of that

extraordinariness she must surrender to fit expectations placed on women. Watching later productions rewrite that balance shows how Broadway's values evolve: the same heroine can reveal something very different about gender depending on the generation telling her story.
