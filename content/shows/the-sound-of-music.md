## Overview

When The Sound of Music opened at the Lunt-Fontanne Theatre on November 16, 1959, it appeared to contain many of the elements audiences associated with Rodgers and Hammerstein: memorable melodies, romance, children, family, and an uplifting belief in human connection. Yet beneath that warmth is a story shaped by political authoritarianism and the moral question of what individuals should do when the world around them changes. Set in Austria in the years leading to the Anschluss of 1938, the musical follows Maria, a young postulant who struggles to adapt to life at Nonnberg Abbey. She is sent to serve as governess to the seven children of the widowed Captain Georg von Trapp, whose household is governed with military discipline. Through music, Maria develops relationships with the children and eventually falls in love with their father. Their private story is gradually overtaken by politics. Nazi Germany annexes Austria, and Captain von Trapp, an Austrian patriot opposed to Nazism, is ordered to accept a commission in the German navy. Instead, the family decides to leave the country.

Songs including “My Favorite Things,” “Do-Re-Mi,” “Edelweiss,” “Climb Ev’ry Mountain,” and the title song became some of the most recognizable music Rodgers and Hammerstein ever wrote. Their familiarity can sometimes obscure the show's central progression: a musical that begins with a woman searching for her place ends with a family deciding what it refuses to become.


## Historical Era

The Sound of Music premiered in 1959, at the end of a remarkable decade for the American musical and at the close of the Rodgers and Hammerstein partnership. Beginning with Oklahoma! in 1943, the pair had helped define Broadway's Golden Age through works including Carousel, South Pacific, and The King and I. The Sound of Music would be their final collaboration. Oscar Hammerstein II died in 1960, less than a year after the production opened. The musical therefore occupies a symbolic position at the end of the Rodgers and Hammerstein era. Its story, however, looks back to Europe on the eve of World War II. In March 1938, Nazi Germany annexed Austria. The production arrived only fourteen years after the war ended, meaning that the political events behind the story remained part of living memory for many members of its original audience.


## Historical & Cultural Context

The musical's political conflict develops gradually. At first, the central questions appear personal: Will Maria remain at the abbey? Can she reach the von Trapp children? Will she acknowledge her feelings for the Captain? But signs of Austria's political crisis increasingly intrude upon that domestic world. Captain von Trapp's friend Max Detweiler is willing to accommodate the new political reality, while the Captain refuses to accept Nazi rule as inevitable. This creates one of the musical's most important contrasts: adaptation versus resistance. The Captain's attachment to Austria is expressed most famously through “Edelweiss.” Although audiences sometimes assume it is a traditional Austrian folk song, Rodgers and Hammerstein wrote it specifically for the musical. Within the story, its meaning changes as the political situation worsens. What begins as an intimate song becomes an expression of attachment to a country that the Captain believes is being taken from him. The musical simplifies elements of the real von Trapp family's history. Maria and Georg had married years before the Anschluss, and the family did not literally escape Austria by hiking over the Alps into Switzerland. The musical transforms historical experience into a more concentrated dramatic narrative. That distinction matters: The Sound of Music is historical storytelling rather than documentary history.


## Production Innovation

Compared with Oklahoma! or West Side Story, The Sound of Music was not formally revolutionary. Its importance instead lies in Rodgers and Hammerstein's extraordinary command of the theatrical language they had helped establish. Music becomes a mechanism for building relationships. “Do-Re-Mi” is not simply a lesson in musical notation; it marks the children's growing connection with Maria. “My Favorite Things” uses imagination and music as a response to fear. “Climb Ev’ry Mountain,” sung by the Mother Abbess, transforms Maria's uncertainty into a larger argument about pursuing one's purpose despite fear. The score also changes meaning through repetition. Music that initially represents childhood, family, or national identity later acquires political significance as the outside world closes in. The title song establishes Maria alone in an open landscape. By the end, the family must leave that landscape behind. The musical's emotional architecture therefore moves from individual freedom, to family belonging, to moral responsibility.


## Historical Significance

The original Broadway production ran for 1,443 performances and won the 1960 Tony Award for Best Musical, tying with Fiorello!. It became one of Rodgers and Hammerstein's final major theatrical successes. Its cultural impact, however, expanded enormously through the 1965 film adaptation starring Julie Andrews and Christopher Plummer. The film became one of the most successful movie musicals ever made and transformed songs from the Broadway score into globally recognizable pieces of popular culture. That success has created an unusual relationship between stage and screen. For many audiences, The Sound of Music is encountered first as a film rather than a Broadway musical, demonstrating how cinema can reshape the historical memory of a theatrical work. The show also marks the conclusion of one of the most influential partnerships in Broadway history. Rodgers and Hammerstein had spent sixteen years expanding the possibilities of musical storytelling. Their works demonstrated that songs, dance, dialogue, design, and dramatic themes could function as parts of a unified theatrical structure. By 1959, however, Broadway was already moving toward another transformation. Gypsy opened the same year, while the following decade would bring Fiddler on the Roof, Cabaret, Hair, and eventually the rise of Stephen Sondheim's concept musicals.

The Sound of Music therefore feels both enduring and transitional: one of the last great monuments of Broadway's Golden Age appearing just as a new theatrical era was beginning.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What interests me most about The Sound of Music is how its meaning changes as the story progresses. Music begins as something personal—a way for Maria to understand herself and connect with the children—but eventually becomes an expression of identity and resistance. That progression makes the musical more than the comforting classic it is sometimes remembered as. It shows how something as joyful as music can become a way of deciding what you value when circumstances force you to choose.
