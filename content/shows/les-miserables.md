## Overview

When Les Misérables opened at the Broadway Theatre on March 12, 1987, it brought Victor Hugo's enormous nineteenth-century novel to the stage as an equally ambitious musical about justice, mercy, poverty, revolution, sacrifice, and the possibility of redemption. At its center is Jean Valjean, imprisoned for stealing bread and repeatedly punished by a legal system that continues to define him by his crime after his release. An act of mercy from a bishop transforms him, and Valjean builds a new identity devoted to helping others. But police inspector Javert refuses to accept that transformation. To Javert, law provides moral certainty: a criminal remains a criminal. Their conflict unfolds across decades of French history and intersects with the lives of Fantine, her daughter Cosette, the young revolutionary Marius, the Thénardiers, Éponine, and the students who build a barricade during the Paris uprising of 1832. Songs including “I Dreamed a Dream,” “Who Am I?,” “Stars,” “On My Own,” “Do You Hear the People Sing?,” “One Day More,” and “Bring Him Home” turn individual struggles into an epic collective story. Despite its revolutionary imagery, however, the musical's deepest conflict is not simply political. It is between two ideas of justice: one based on rigid judgment and another based on the possibility that people can change.


## Historical Era

Les Misérables reached Broadway in 1987, after an unusual international journey. The musical began as a French-language concept album in 1980 and was staged in Paris that year. Producer Cameron Mackintosh later worked with Boublil and Schönberg to substantially develop the piece for an English-language production, which opened in London in 1985 before transferring to Broadway. Its arrival placed it at the center of the 1980s megamusical era. Like Cats and The Phantom of the Opera, Les Misérables offered audiences an emotionally expansive score, monumental staging, a distinctive visual identity, and a production capable of traveling internationally. Yet its spectacle served an unusually serious story.

Instead of fantasy or Gothic romance, Les Misérables presented poverty, prostitution, political violence, imprisonment, child exploitation, and death on a scale rarely associated with commercial musical theatre.


## Historical & Cultural Context

Despite a common misconception, Les Misérables is not about the French Revolution of 1789. Most of the musical takes place decades later, culminating in the June Rebellion of 1832, a short-lived republican uprising in Paris against the monarchy of King Louis-Philippe. This distinction matters because the students at the barricade are not participating in an inevitable national revolution. Their uprising fails. That historical failure changes the meaning of their idealism. They fight without knowing whether anyone will join them, and most die without seeing the political transformation they hoped to create. The broader world of the musical reflects the severe social inequalities Hugo examined in his novel. Fantine's collapse from factory worker to prostitution demonstrates how quickly poverty can become catastrophic for someone without social protection. Valjean's original imprisonment for stealing bread exposes the disproportionate punishment of poverty. Hugo's central concern was therefore not simply revolution but the systems that determine who society considers worthy of dignity.


## Production Innovation

Les Misérables is almost entirely sung through, using recurring melodies and musical themes to connect characters across an enormous narrative. The score repeatedly reuses musical material in new contexts. A melody associated with one character can return with different lyrics for another, creating emotional connections across seemingly separate stories. The structure reaches its most spectacular expression in “One Day More.” Nearly every major character sings about what the coming day means to them: Valjean anticipates flight, Javert prepares to infiltrate the rebels, Marius and Cosette face separation, Éponine confronts unrequited love, the students prepare for revolution, and the Thénardiers anticipate profit. Different melodies and motivations gradually combine into a single ensemble. The song makes something extraordinarily complex immediately understandable: everyone is moving toward the same historical moment for entirely different reasons. The original staging also became famous for its revolving stage. The turntable allowed streets, barricades, bodies, and crowds to move continuously through space, giving the production a cinematic sense of momentum.

The barricade itself became one of the defining images of the megamusical.


## Historical Significance

Les Misérables became one of the most successful musicals in theatrical history. The original Broadway production ran for 6,680 performances, from 1987 to 2003, and won eight Tony Awards, including the 1987 Tony Award for Best Musical. Its international reach has been even greater. The London production has continued through multiple theaters and revisions, making Les Misérables one of the world's longest-running musicals. The show's logo—the image of young Cosette adapted from an illustration connected to Hugo's novel—became a globally recognizable piece of theatrical branding. Along with Cats and The Phantom of the Opera, Les Misérables demonstrated that a musical could become a global cultural product, reproduced across languages and countries while retaining a recognizable musical and visual identity. Yet its longevity cannot be explained by spectacle alone. At its center is the relationship between Valjean and Javert. Javert believes identity is fixed: once someone has broken the law, that act defines him. Valjean's life becomes evidence for the opposite possibility. The turning point comes when Valjean has the opportunity to kill Javert but instead releases him. Javert can understand punishment; he cannot understand mercy from the man he has spent his life pursuing. His worldview collapses because Valjean behaves in a way his moral system cannot explain.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What stays with me most about Les Misérables is the conflict between Valjean and Javert. Both men are extraordinarily committed to what they believe is right, but one worldview allows people to change while the other requires them to remain defined by their worst action. The musical's scale is enormous—revolutions, barricades, crowds, decades of history—but its most powerful idea is surprisingly personal: sometimes one act of mercy can change the direction of another person's entire life. That tension between systems and individuals is what makes Les Misérables feel much larger than a historical epic.
