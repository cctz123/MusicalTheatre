## Overview

When Of Thee I Sing opened at the Music Box Theatre on December 26, 1931, it brought something unusual to Broadway: a musical comedy built around a sustained satire of American presidential politics. Rather than treating politics as background material for a conventional romance, the show turned elections, political parties, the presidency, the Supreme Court, and the rituals of American democracy themselves into the joke. The story follows John P. Wintergreen, a presidential candidate whose campaign adopts the deliberately meaningless slogan “Love.” As a publicity stunt, his party organizes a national beauty contest and promises that Wintergreen will marry the winner. Instead, he falls in love with Mary Turner, a contestant who wins his affection with her corn muffins. His decision to marry Mary rather than the official contest winner, Diana Devereaux, escalates from romantic farce into an international diplomatic crisis.

The absurdity is intentional. Of Thee I Sing presents American politics as spectacle: candidates are marketed, voters are manipulated, ceremonies substitute for substance, and supposedly serious institutions behave according to the logic of musical comedy.


## Historical Era

The musical arrived during the Great Depression, two years after the 1929 stock-market crash and less than a year before the presidential election of 1932. Confidence in American political and economic institutions was under enormous pressure. Broadway was changing as well. The sophisticated musical comedies of the 1920s remained popular, but writers increasingly demonstrated that musical theatre could engage directly with contemporary American life. Of Thee I Sing belongs to this transitional moment. Like Show Boat before it, the production helped expand what a musical could address, although it did so through satire rather than dramatic realism. Its political irreverence was particularly striking. The president, vice president, Congress, Supreme Court, political parties, and diplomatic establishment were not treated as sacred symbols of government. They became characters and institutions that audiences were invited to laugh at.


## Historical & Cultural Context

The title itself comes from the patriotic song “My Country, ’Tis of Thee,” immediately signaling the show's playful relationship with American nationalism. Throughout the musical, familiar symbols of patriotism are exaggerated until they become absurd. That satire reflected a broader culture increasingly aware of the machinery behind modern politics. Campaign slogans, publicity, personality, and mass media had become central features of presidential elections. Of Thee I Sing asks what happens when those mechanisms become more important than political substance. Wintergreen campaigns on “Love” precisely because the word means almost anything. The joke feels remarkably modern: the campaign does not necessarily need a coherent ideology if it can produce an appealing candidate and an emotionally effective message. The musical therefore turns democracy itself into theatrical performance. Elections resemble entertainment, politicians become performers, and citizens become an audience being persuaded to applaud.


## Production Innovation

The show's greatest innovation was the degree to which its music, lyrics, book, and satire operated as a unified whole. George and Ira Gershwin were already among Broadway's most successful songwriting teams, but Of Thee I Sing used their songs within an unusually cohesive political narrative. Numbers such as “Who

Cares?,” “Love Is Sweeping the Country,” and the title song simultaneously function as memorable popular music and parodies of political rhetoric. The production also blurred distinctions between musical comedy and political commentary. Instead of stopping the story for isolated topical jokes, Kaufman and Ryskind constructed an entire fictional political system whose escalating absurdity drives the plot. In this sense, Of Thee I Sing represents another step toward the increasingly integrated American musical. Where Show Boat demonstrated that songs could support serious dramatic storytelling, Of Thee I Sing showed that the same integration could sustain intellectual and political satire.


## Historical Significance

In 1932, Of Thee I Sing became the first musical to win the Pulitzer Prize for Drama. That achievement was more than an award for one successful production. It signaled that musical theatre could be recognized as serious American dramatic writing rather than simply commercial entertainment. Notably, the Pulitzer was awarded to book writers George S. Kaufman and Morrie Ryskind and lyricist Ira Gershwin, emphasizing the show's literary and satirical achievements. Its influence can be seen in the long tradition of politically engaged American musicals that followed. Broadway would increasingly become a place where creators could examine presidents, elections, capitalism, race, war, nationalism, and the contradictions of American democracy through music and comedy. Of Thee I Sing therefore marks an important expansion of Broadway's possibilities. The musical did not merely tell a story set in America; it used the musical itself to interrogate how America tells stories about its own democracy.


## Why It Resonates With Me

What fascinates me about Of Thee I Sing is how contemporary its central idea still feels. Politics is presented not only as government, but as communication, branding, performance, and storytelling. Nearly a century later, the mechanisms have changed, but the question underneath the satire remains recognizable: when politics becomes a performance, how do we distinguish persuasion from substance?
