## Immigration

Who Gets to Call America Home? Immigration is woven throughout the history of American musical theater because immigration is woven throughout the history of America itself. Broadway was shaped by immigrant artists, particularly Jewish composers, lyricists, producers, and performers whose families had arrived from Europe. Yet for decades, immigrant experiences were often transformed into generalized stories of becoming American rather than examined directly. Fiddler on the Roof changed that relationship.

Tevye's family is forced from Anatevka, making displacement central to the musical's conclusion. The audience knows something the characters cannot: families like theirs will scatter across Europe and America, carrying traditions into new countries. In Ragtime, immigration becomes part of a larger portrait of early twentieth-century America. Tateh arrives as a struggling Jewish immigrant and eventually reinvents himself within the American entertainment industry. His story exists beside those of Black Americans and wealthy white families, revealing that the supposedly unified American experience is actually composed of radically unequal ones. In the Heights moves the question forward another century. Its characters are not simply arriving in America. They have built a community in Washington Heights and must now confront another question: Can you belong somewhere even when economic forces make it increasingly difficult to remain there? Broadway's immigration stories therefore evolve alongside America's. The question moves from How do we become American? to Who gets to define what American means?

What I take from this is not a progress narrative. It is a change in the question. Early musicals often treat arrival as the plot. Later ones treat staying—paying rent, keeping a block, remaining visible—as the harder act. That is a historical argument about the country, not a preference among scores.
