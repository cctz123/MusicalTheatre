## Inequality

Who Gets the American Dream? Perhaps the most persistent contradiction running through American musicals is the distance between the promise of opportunity and the unequal reality of who receives it. West Side Story places young people inside neighborhoods divided by race, ethnicity, poverty, and territorial conflict.

A Chorus Line reveals the economic vulnerability underneath theatrical glamour: dozens of extraordinarily talented performers compete for a handful of jobs. Rent places artists within a New York transformed by AIDS, poverty, and gentrification. In the Heights asks whether a community can survive when the people who created it can no longer afford to remain. The Outsiders makes class difference visible through teenagers: the Greasers and Socs live in the same city but inhabit radically different versions of America. And Hamilton turns the American promise into its central contradiction. Alexander Hamilton arrives with almost nothing and rises extraordinarily far. But the musical also asks who gets remembered, who gets excluded, and who controls the narrative afterward. The American Dream has always been powerful partly because it promises movement: you can become something other than what you were born into. Broadway repeatedly tests that promise against reality.

Hamilton rises. The chorus of A Chorus Line mostly does not. Rent’s loft is already being priced out. In the Heights asks whether success means leaving the block that made you. The Dream is not a theme Broadway celebrates. It is a claim Broadway cross-examines.
