## Gender

Who Is Allowed to Want More? Broadway heroines have always wanted things. But what they are permitted to want has changed. Earlier musicals frequently organized women's stories around romance and marriage. Over time, female characters increasingly demanded ambitions that existed beyond romantic fulfillment. Rose in Gypsy wants success with an almost terrifying intensity. Eva Perón in Evita wants political and cultural power. Elphaba in Wicked refuses the identity society assigns her. The women of Suffs demand political citizenship. The six wives of SIX reclaim historical identities traditionally defined through their relationships to Henry VIII. Revivals make these changing expectations even more visible. When Bobby became Bobbie in the 2021 Company, the same questions about being unmarried at thirty-five acquired different meanings because the protagonist was now a woman. Gender on Broadway therefore exists not only inside stories. It shapes how audiences interpret ambition, independence, marriage, power, aging, and success.

The 2021 Company is the cleanest proof. The plot barely changes. The permission does. A woman asking whether she wants to be married is not the same cultural object as a man asking the same question in 1970. Broadway’s archive of gender is as much in revivals as in premieres.
