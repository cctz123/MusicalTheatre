By the mid-1970s, Broadway stood at another turning point. The intimate, psychologically complex musicals of the previous decade had expanded the artistic possibilities of the form, but the economics of theatre were changing rapidly. Rising production costs, increasing international investment, and advances in stage technology encouraged producers to think on an unprecedented scale. The result was the emergence of the mega-musical—a new model of theatrical production that transformed Broadway into a global entertainment industry. Unlike earlier musicals, mega-musicals were designed not simply as individual productions but as international brands. Lavish scenery, groundbreaking stage technology, elaborate costumes, spectacular

special effects, and emotionally sweeping scores became defining characteristics. These productions often ran for decades, toured worldwide, and were recreated with remarkable consistency across continents, making Broadway one of the first truly global forms of live entertainment. Much of this movement was driven by British producers and composers. Andrew Lloyd Webber, Cameron Mackintosh, Trevor Nunn, Claude-Michel Schönberg, Alain Boublil, and their collaborators reshaped commercial musical theatre by combining cinematic storytelling with large-scale theatrical spectacle. Revolving stages, falling chandeliers, automated scenery, helicopter effects, and sophisticated lighting systems became attractions in their own right, expanding audiences' expectations of what could be achieved on stage. Yet spectacle alone did not explain the success of these productions. Beneath their visual grandeur lay universal stories of revolution, love, sacrifice, memory, political conflict, and personal redemption. Whether set during the French Revolution, the Vietnam War, nineteenth-century Paris, or the presidential palace of Argentina, these musicals explored themes that resonated across cultures and languages, helping Broadway reach audiences around the world. This era also witnessed the growing globalization of the theatre industry itself. Productions became increasingly expensive, requiring multinational financing, international licensing agreements, sophisticated marketing campaigns, and long-term strategic planning. Broadway shifted from a predominantly American institution into the center of a worldwide theatrical network. At the same time, American composers continued to innovate. Stephen Sondheim produced some of his most artistically ambitious works during these years, demonstrating that Broadway could support both commercially spectacular productions and intellectually challenging musical theatre. The coexistence of blockbuster entertainment and artistic experimentation remains one of the defining characteristics of Broadway today.
